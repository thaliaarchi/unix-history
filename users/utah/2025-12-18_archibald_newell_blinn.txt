Hi Thalia
I will be happy to answer any questions, that is, those that i actually have answers to ☺

I don’t know if you got my response email to an earlier question. Here’s a copy below if not.
The punchline is that I never encountered Unix at Utah. The frame buffer and PDP11 used DECs operating system.
Just as an addition to this email was my impression that the Unix file system (as used in 1976 at NYIT) was very fragile. I recall our colleague Christie Barton spending much time “repairing I-nodes”

Also about some  questions below:
The PDP11 with the frame buffer had no electronic connection to the PDP10 as i remember. I did use the PDP10 for email and got some data from Nelson Max at Bowling Green through this email. I believe i transferred that data to the PDP11 using a magnetic tape, also known as “sneaker net”.

You can probably get more info on the usage of Unix at NYIT from either Ed Catmull or Alvy Ray Smith. I can probably put you in touch with them. Unix was definitely in use there the summer of 1976 when i visited. I wrote up my memories of that summer in an IEEE article which i can dig up.

The PDP11 had an E&S Picture System 2, not a PS-1. The “random access picture store” was the frame buffer. The PS-2 was a separate device that only did line drawings with a different calligraphic display. It had hardware to do matrix multiplication to do real time 3D line drawings. Unix was, as i mentioned, not involved with this particular hardware at Utah.

The first use of the Newell teapot was in Martin’s thesis. The Texture and Reflection paper was after that, but was more widely distributed so helped introduce the teapot to a larger audience.

I don’t recall any rear-projection wall in the machine room. Just the monitors to see the images.

One of the staff of maintance/programmers was Duane Palyka.


Here's what i remember.
>
> When i got to Utah in September 1974 the frame buffer was still being
> debugged on the PDP11 by Jim Kajiya. The operating system was whatever
> DEC distributed with the hardware, i believe it was just called DOS
> (nothing to do with the PC DOS system) for "Disk Operating System".
> The main programming language was Fortran. The operating system was
> fairly simple and did not even use protected mode. That made it easy
> to access the new hardware though. Sometime over the next year the
> system was upgraded to RSX-11M, DECs protected mode system that
> required special drivers to be installed to access the frame buffer. I
> never saw Unix used on the machine. The first time i saw Unix was the
> summer of 1976 when i visited New York Institute of Technology and
> used Unix and C there. But I never used Unix or C at Utah. I was
> somewhat unimpressed with Unix at the time because it was so flakey.
> It couldn't handle magnetic tapes properly without crashing the
> machine, and the C compiler had a bug that caused it to generate bad
> binaries in some cases. It had to do with the PDP11 short jump
> instruction being used when it shouldn't have been. But i found out later that that was really the early days of Unix so it was still pretty raw.
