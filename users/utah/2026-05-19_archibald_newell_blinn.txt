Hi Martin and Jim,

Hope you're doing well. That UNIX V4 tape has continued to drive interest and I
was invited to give a talk at SIGGRAPH about the tape and its small connection
to your research. Erik Brunvand says you'll be at SIGGRAPH? I'd love to see you
at my talk, tentatively scheduled for first thing Thursday morning.

Ironically, this is my first publication, not something related to my "actual"
research in compiler verification. Since I'm not a graphics person, I have
imposter syndrome for SIGGRAPH. And essentially whatever I say becomes "fact",
since I'm the only one who's dug into this, so I want to get it as close as I
can. I'd appreciate your feedback and corrections. Your comments from last time
have been very helpful.


In preparing, I'm going through my narrative to make sure it's solid. Here's
what I have:

Around 1974, two systems were in use for computer graphics at the U. Your
(Martin) thesis in procedural modeling was done on a PDP-10. And from 1973 to
1975, you were responsible for setting up a new graphics facility based on a
PDP-11/45 with Jim and Duane Palyka.

This version of UNIX only ran the PDP-11/45 and /40, so the June 1974 UNIX tape
would have been for the new lab. But UNIX was probably only briefly tried, not
used for any work, and abandoned in favor of DEC DOS, then RSX-11M.

If the work for your thesis was only on the PDP-10, then there was no technical
overlap between the teapot and UNIX. Though the Blinn-ratio teapot in Texture
and Reflection (1976) was on the 11/45, it was slightly later. But, Man Machine
Communication (March 1974) was on the 11/45, the right time and computer. I need
to learn more about that paper.

I have less understanding of E&S hardware and graphics in general. Jim said the
PDP-11 had a PS-2. I don't know what E&S hardware the PDP-10 had.

As a twist, you weren't the only one from the U to have early interest in UNIX.
Dr. Paul Abegglen considered UNIX in November 1973 for the mass spectroscopy
data gathering system he was building for the Chemistry Department, seven months
before you. His doctorate was in Computer Science, completed around 1972. He was
30 years old, possibly teaching as a professor. I had thought a connection to
you was remote, but an E&S interactive graphics system was installed on a
Chemistry PDP-11/45 in 1977. Perhaps they collaborated with you before then.


I think the picture is becoming more clear to me. I didn't understand which
projects were using which machines until writing this.

I have some follow-up questions:

- In our last conversation, you said that you modeled the teapot definitely
  before 1975, best guess being mid-1974. You said you might have something in
  your archives that could pin it down. Did you have a chance to take a look?

- Do you have Duane Palyka's contact information? I'm curious to hear his
  recollections.

- Do you have photos of the graphics facilities or your projects at the time? Of
  the PDP-11/45, PDP-10, or PS-2? It would be fun to show those in my talk.

- Were the models in Procedure Models (1975) not real-time? Was this a
  distinguishing feature of the PDP-11/45 system compared to the PDP-10? An
  August 1973 E&S proposal describes a project led by Martin to build a
  real-time shaded picture generating system on a PDP-11/45.

- We take it for granted today, but wasn't Procedure Models (1975) the
  first time models were described with rules instead of data sets? Also the
  first time Bezíer curves were applied to graphics?


Thank you so much!

Thalia Archibald

Here's what Google says about Bezier curves:

"Bézier curves are parametric curves used in computer graphics and design, named after French engineer Pierre Bézier, who popularized them in the 1960s while working at Renault to design car bodies.  However, the mathematical foundation lies in the Bernstein polynomials established by Sergei Natanovich Bernstein in 1912.
The curves were first developed for computer-aided design in 1959 by mathematician Paul de Casteljau at Citroën, who created the de Casteljau’s algorithm for evaluating them.  De Casteljau’s work remained confidential until the 1980s, whereas Bézier independently discovered similar methods and publicly published his findings in 1962, leading to the curves being named after him."
